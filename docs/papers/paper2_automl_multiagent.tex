\title{Clinically-Enriched AutoML with Multi-Agent LLM\\ Reporting for Biomedical Signal Classification:\\ Design and a Critical Methodological Evaluation}

\begin{abstract}
Accuracy alone is a poor summary of a biomedical classifier: a model that
misses malignant cases can still look excellent on paper. We present the
decision layer of \emph{BioStatusIA}, an open clinical decision-support system,
which combines (i) an automated machine-learning (AutoML) module that compares
six classifiers under a clinically-enriched metric set---sensitivity,
specificity, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), Cohen's $\kappa$, expected
calibration error (ECE) and a McNemar A/B test---with class balancing
(SMOTE/ADASYN) and SHAP-based interpretability, and (ii) a multi-agent layer of
five CrewAI crews driven by a locally-hosted large language model that turns the
numerical output into a structured radiology-style report. Beyond describing the
design, the central contribution of this paper is a \emph{critical
self-audit} of the methodology: we identify and quantify four concrete
threats to validity---normalization computed before the train/test split,
oversampling applied before cross-validation, an inadvertently disabled
sensitivity floor in model selection, and single-split point estimates on small
datasets---and we contrast optimistic synthetic results (AUC$=$1.0) with the
honest operating point on real breast-ultrasound data (AUC$\approx$0.80,
recall$\approx$0.45). We argue that publishing such an audit is itself good
practice for reproducible medical AI.
\end{abstract}

AutoML, clinical machine learning, model calibration, class imbalance, SHAP,

\section{Introduction}
The temptation to report a single accuracy number is especially dangerous in
medicine. On an imbalanced screening dataset a classifier that always predicts
``benign'' can achieve high accuracy while being clinically useless, because it
never raises the alarm on the cases that matter. A trustworthy medical
decision-support system therefore needs (a) a metric suite that exposes
sensitivity, calibration and agreement, not just accuracy; (b) an explicit
account of how the winning model was chosen; and (c) a transparent statement of
the methodological choices that could bias the reported numbers.

This paper describes and critically evaluates the decision layer of
\emph{BioStatusIA}. The companion paper covers ingestion and feature extraction
across four data families; here we take the extracted feature vectors as input
and study two subsystems built on top of them. The first is an AutoML module
that trains and compares six classifiers under a clinically-enriched protocol.
The second is a multi-agent reporting layer in which specialized software agents,
each backed by a large language model (LLM), transform the numerical results
into a human-readable clinical narrative under a strict ethical template.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we specify a clinically-enriched
evaluation protocol and the model-selection rule that a screening context
demands. Second, we describe a multi-agent architecture that separates
quantitative analysis from natural-language reporting, keeping the LLM away from
the numbers it must not invent. Third---and most importantly---we perform a
\emph{methodological self-audit}, naming and quantifying the biases in our own
pipeline, so that the reported results can be interpreted correctly and
corrected in future work.

\section{Related Work}
Automated machine learning seeks to remove manual trial-and-error from model
selection \cite{he2021automl}. In clinical settings the emphasis shifts from raw
accuracy toward metrics that reflect diagnostic risk: sensitivity and
specificity, the Matthews correlation coefficient---shown to be more
informative than F1 and accuracy on imbalanced data \cite{chicco2020mcc}---and
Cohen's $\kappa$ for chance-corrected agreement \cite{cohen1960kappa}.
Calibration, the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed
frequencies, is frequently neglected yet critical when a probability is shown to
a clinician \cite{guo2017calibration}. Class imbalance is commonly addressed
with synthetic oversampling such as SMOTE \cite{chawla2002smote} and ADASYN
\cite{he2008adasyn}, implemented here through imbalanced-learn
\cite{lemaitre2017imblearn}; a well-known pitfall is that oversampling must be
performed \emph{inside} each cross-validation fold to avoid optimistic bias
\cite{santos2018crossval}. Model comparison should be tested for significance,
for which McNemar's test is the standard on a shared test set
\cite{dietterich1998}. Interpretability is provided by SHAP values
\cite{lundberg2017shap}. Finally, LLM-based multi-agent frameworks such as
CrewAI have made it practical to compose role-specialized agents; their use in
medicine, however, raises well-documented hallucination and safety concerns
\cite{ji2023hallucination}. Our work sits at the intersection of these threads
and, unusually, foregrounds the failure modes rather than hiding them.

\section{The AutoML Decision Layer}
\subsection{Model pool and protocol}
Six classifiers compete on every labelled dataset: logistic regression,
$k$-nearest neighbours, an RBF support-vector machine, a random forest, gradient
boosting, and a multilayer perceptron. Features are standardized, the data are
split into an 80/20 stratified holdout, and each model is additionally assessed
with stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training partition. For every
fold and for the final holdout we record accuracy, sensitivity (recall of the
positive class), specificity, precision, F1, ROC-AUC, MCC, Cohen's $\kappa$,
ECE over ten bins, inference latency and training time. The two leading models
are compared with a continuity-corrected McNemar test that reports
$\chi^2$, a $p$-value, and a significance flag at $\alpha=0.05$.

\subsection{Class balancing and interpretability}
When the training partition is imbalanced and the minority class has enough
neighbours, SMOTE (or ADASYN) resamples it; the procedure fails safe, returning
the original data when resampling is not viable, and---by construction---is
applied only to the training partition so that the held-out test set remains
untouched. The winning model is explained with SHAP: a \texttt{TreeExplainer}
for tree ensembles and a sampled \texttt{KernelExplainer} otherwise, yielding a
global ranking of feature importance that feeds both the interface and the
population report.

\subsection{Model-selection rule}
The intended rule reflects screening priorities: among models above a minimum
sensitivity, pick the highest AUC, so that the system prefers catching disease
over marginal overall accuracy. The winner is persisted to disk for single-case
inference, together with its scaler, feature names and metrics.

\section{Multi-Agent Reporting Layer}
Numbers are necessary but not sufficient for clinicians, so BioStatusIA adds a
narrative layer built from five CrewAI crews and eight role-specialized agents
orchestrated sequentially and backed by a locally-hosted LLM
(Ollama, \texttt{qwen2.5}). An imaging pipeline chains a PDI engineer, a
technical analyst, a data scientist and a radiologist agent; parallel crews
handle tabular data, physiological signals (F1) and medical images (F3/F4);
a fifth, tool-free crew produces a focused single-case report. Crucially, agents
communicate through the filesystem: each tool persists a complete JSON artifact
and returns only a short textual summary to the LLM, so the language model
composes prose \emph{around} numbers it cannot alter. The interactive report is
constrained to five mandatory sections---principal finding, severity 1--5,
comparison to reference, immediate recommendation, and an ethical
disclaimer---and the reporting agents are deliberately given no analytical
tools, preventing them from fabricating measurements.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Real imaging: the honest operating point}
Table~\ref{tab:busi} reports the six-model comparison on 780 breast-ultrasound
images (570 benign, 210 malignant). The random forest is selected on AUC
(0.800) with the best accuracy (0.814), but its recall of 0.452 is the headline
clinical result: on genuinely imbalanced data the default image branch misses
more than half of the malignant cases. This is exactly the kind of number that a
raw-accuracy report would bury, and it directly motivates moving class balancing
into the default path.

\subsection{Small and synthetic data: optimistic scores}
On a 100-image balanced mini-set the MLP reached AUC~0.94 with sensitivity and
specificity of 0.90. On a 50-row tabular panel and on 14-example synthetic
ECG/DICOM/NIfTI sets, several models reached AUC~1.0 with perfect sensitivity,
specificity, MCC and $\kappa$. We report these numbers precisely because they
are \emph{not} evidence of clinical performance: perfect scores on datasets with
only a handful of test examples reflect the ease of separating synthetic or
tiny samples, amplified by the methodological factors analyzed next.

\section{Critical Methodological Evaluation}
This section is the core contribution. We audited the implementation and
identified four threats to validity, ordered by clinical impact.

\subsection{T1 --- Normalization leakage}
The feature scaler is fit on the \emph{entire} dataset before the train/test
split, so test-set statistics influence the normalization of the training data.
This is a textbook form of data leakage that inflates reported performance. The
correct remedy is to fit the scaler on the training partition only---ideally by
wrapping the scaler and estimator in a single pipeline so the fit is confined to
each split.

\subsection{T2 --- Oversampling before cross-validation}
SMOTE is applied to the training partition, after which stratified folds are
drawn from the already-oversampled set. Synthetic points can then be generated
from neighbours that later land in a validation fold, leaking information across
folds and optimistically biasing the cross-validated metrics
\cite{santos2018crossval}. Oversampling belongs \emph{inside} each fold, e.g.\
via an imbalanced-learn pipeline evaluated with cross-validation.

\subsection{T3 --- Disabled clinical selection floor}
The stated selection rule prefers models with sensitivity $\geq 0.8$; in the
implementation the guard is written so that the sensitivity condition is
short-circuited and every model qualifies, reducing the rule to pure
maximum-AUC. On BUSI this is why a model with recall~0.452 is selected. The fix
is a one-line correction, but its clinical consequence---false negatives in
cancer screening---is severe, which is precisely why the audit matters.

\subsection{T4 --- Single-split point estimates on small data}
All headline test metrics come from one fixed 80/20 split. On the 50-row and
14-example sets the test partition contains a handful of cases, so a single
misclassification swings AUC dramatically and the reported 1.0 values carry no
confidence interval. Repeated or nested cross-validation with reported variance,
and DeLong intervals on AUC, are needed before any performance claim.

\subsection{Secondary observations}
The McNemar baseline in code compares the winner against the first model rather
than the second-best, diverging from the documented ``two best models'' intent;
GLCM texture uses a single distance and orientation; and the LLM reporting layer
is not validated for factual grounding---indeed the local model produced
multi-hour timeouts in our environment, so reports were generated from the
deterministic numeric artifacts. None of these invalidate the architecture, but
each must be disclosed.

\section{Discussion}
Two lessons emerge. First, a rich metric suite is only as trustworthy as the
protocol that produces it: MCC, $\kappa$ and ECE are meaningless if the split
leaks or the selection rule is silently disabled. Second, separating the
numeric engine from the LLM narrator is a sound safety pattern---the language
model cannot alter measurements it never touches---but it does not by itself
make the narrative correct, and an unvalidated 3-billion-parameter model must
not be trusted for clinical wording. The remediation roadmap is concrete and
inexpensive: move scaling and oversampling inside cross-validation, restore the
sensitivity floor, replace single-split estimates with repeated CV and
confidence intervals, and add a factual-grounding check to the reporting agents.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
BioStatusIA is a decision-support tool, not a diagnostic authority. A mandatory
disclaimer that its output does not replace a licensed physician appears in
every screen and every report, including as a required section of each LLM
narrative. The self-audit in this paper is itself an ethical instrument: by
publishing the ways in which our numbers can mislead, we reduce the risk that a
reader over-trusts them. We consider the honest reporting of a recall of 0.452
more valuable than a polished but leaky 1.0.

\section{Conclusion}
We described the AutoML and multi-agent reporting layers of BioStatusIA and
subjected them to a critical methodological audit. The system contributes a
clinically-enriched evaluation protocol and a safety-oriented separation between
numeric analysis and LLM reporting; the audit contributes something rarer---a
transparent, quantified account of four validity threats in our own pipeline and
their fixes. We advocate this audit-first posture as a norm for reproducible
medical machine learning, where the cost of an unexamined optimistic result is
measured in missed diagnoses.
